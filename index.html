Suspension Limit Straps — What Serious Builders Actually Run

If you know what a limiting strap is, you already know why you need one. This is the guide for prerunner trucks, desert racers, long-travel builds, UTVs, and rock crawlers — with a real look at the brand that supplies them and the builds they go on.

Updated: August 2026  ·  Prerunner · UTV · Desert Truck · Rock Crawler · Jeep · Bronco applications

Limit straps aren't a beginner modification. They're a fundamental component of any finished suspension build that sees real droop travel. A stock Tacoma with a two-inch spacer lift doesn't need them. A long-travel prerunner running 18 inches of travel, a Carli-equipped Ram 2500 running desert, or a Can-Am Maverick X3 on the dunes absolutely does. The strap goes between the chassis and the suspension — and its job is to be the last line of defense before your CV axle, brake line, or shock shaft decides to be the hard stop instead.

The physics: At full droop — wheel in a rut, one corner light at speed — your CV axle hits its maximum bind angle, your shock shaft hits its full extension, and your brake line reaches maximum stretch. Without a limit strap, one of those three is your suspension's mechanical stop. With a limit strap set correctly, none of them ever gets there.

How to Size Limit Straps for Long-Travel Builds

Generic sizing guides aimed at mall crawlers are useless for serious builds. Here's how builders actually approach it:

Step 1 — Establish True Full Droop

Jack the vehicle and let the suspension hang fully. Measure the distance from your intended upper mounting point (frame rail, chassis tab, upper shock mount area) to your intended lower mounting point (axle housing, lower control arm) at absolute full extension. This is your maximum strap-deployed length.

Step 2 — Find the Protection Point

Identify which component — CV axle, brake line, or shock shaft — reaches its safe limit first. On IFS trucks and UTVs, the CV axle bind angle is typically the constraint. On solid axle builds, it's usually the brake line routing. Mark the droop point at which that component reaches its limit.

Step 3 — Subtract Your Safety Margin

Subtract 1–2 inches from the maximum-protection-point measurement to set the strap's catch position before the component reaches its hard limit. For high-speed desert applications where the suspension hits full droop under dynamic load, go tighter — you're catching it at speed, not slowly. For rock crawling where speed is controlled, you have more latitude.

Common Strap Lengths by Build Type

  • Carli Pintop (Ram 2500 / F-250): 22–24" front, verified on Carli's own documented fitment data
  • Carli Backcountry 2.0: 18–20" front
  • Long-travel prerunner (Tacoma, 14–18" travel): 18–24" depending on coilover and UCA geometry
  • Can-Am Maverick X3 stock travel: 14–16" — verify against your specific control arm setup
  • Jeep Wrangler JL / JK at 3.5": 15–17"

Always verify: These are starting points. Jack one corner fully after installation and confirm the strap catches before your CV or brake line reaches its limit. The five-minute post-install check is the only way to confirm correct sizing on your specific setup. Don't skip it.

Installation Notes for Serious Builds

Prerunner and desert truck installations differ from standard trail-rig installs in a few important ways:

Hardware grade matters more at speed. A limit strap on a desert truck hits its catch point under dynamic load at 50+ mph, not at controlled rock-crawling speeds. The hardware at both ends — clevis pins, bolts, tabs — needs to be rated for dynamic shock loading, not just static weight. Use grade-8 hardware minimum at all mounting points on high-speed applications.

Tab position affects strap angle. On a prerunner with long upper control arms and a wide track, the angle of the strap at full droop changes relative to the force direction. A strap catching at an extreme angle concentrates load on one side of the hardware. On custom builds, fabricate tabs that put the strap as close to vertical as possible at full droop to distribute the catch load evenly through the hardware and webbing.

Inspect after every event. Desert racing and high-speed off-road use puts limit straps through dynamic loading that trail use doesn't. After every race or fast desert run, inspect the webbing for any fraying at the edges or near the hardware end, and check for any deformation of the clevis hardware. A strap that catches a single hard droop at speed should be inspected before the next event — not just the next year.

Solid Axle vs IFS — Different Approach, Same Goal

How you mount the strap depends on your suspension configuration:

Solid front axle (Jeep Wrangler, Ram 2500, Ford Super Duty 2017+) — one strap per side from the frame rail above the axle to the axle housing or a dedicated tab on the diff cover. The axle moves as a unit, so both sides droop together. Mounting is typically straightforward — there's a clear lower mounting point at the axle housing.

Independent front suspension (Tacoma, Tundra, Ford F-150, Bronco, most UTVs) — each corner droops independently, and each corner gets its own strap from the frame to the lower control arm. IFS installations require more care to identify the correct lower mounting position — it needs to be on the lower control arm in a location that catches the arm at the correct droop angle without interfering with the ball joint pivot or the control arm's range of motion.

UTV long-travel — similar to IFS trucks, but factory UTV control arms often have less room for tab options. Bull Strap's 9/16" hardware is sized to work with the standard clevis bolt patterns found on most UTV aftermarket lower A-arm hardware. If you're running custom arms, verify tab size before ordering.

FAQ

What are suspension limit straps?
Webbing straps that connect your frame or chassis to a suspension component — axle housing or control arm — at a fixed length. When the suspension droops fully, the strap catches and stops further travel before CV axles, brake lines, or shock shafts reach their mechanical limits. On any build with significant droop travel — long-travel prerunner, UTV, desert truck, rock crawler, or lifted 4x4 — they're a fundamental part of a complete suspension setup.
Do UTVs need limit straps?
Yes. Can-Am Maverick X3, Polaris RZR Pro XP, Yamaha YXZ, and similar long-travel UTV platforms have droop ranges that can damage CV axles and shock seals without a droop stop. Bull Strap limit straps use 9/16" hardware compatible with standard UTV suspension tabs.
How do I size limit straps for a prerunner or desert truck?
Measure from upper to lower mounting point at full droop, then subtract 1–2 inches to set the catch point. On long-travel setups running 14–20 inches of travel, front straps are typically 18–24 inches. Verify by jacking one corner and confirming the strap goes taut before any component reaches its limit. Carli Pintop Ram: 22–24". Carli Backcountry: 18–20". Long-travel Tacoma prerunner: 18–24" depending on geometry.

Limit straps vs bump stops — what's the difference?
Bump stops limit compression travel — they prevent the chassis from bottoming into the axle or control arm. Limit straps limit droop travel — they prevent the suspension from extending beyond a safe point when a wheel lifts. On any serious off-road build, both are part of a complete suspension setup. They solve different problems at opposite ends of the suspension travel range.
